In this paper, I explore the relationship between the turn position and meaning of the 
adverb/particle combination “so yeah.” By applying conversation analysis methodology to data 
consisting of recorded every-day phone calls and face-to-face interactions, I examine the features 
and functions of the English “so yeah” in different turn positions.  
Existing work shows that a prosodically marked “so” can be used to project an upshot 
that takes on particular meanings in different positions (Raymond, 2004)—the “turn-final so” 
projects an upshot that the speaker will not go on to produce, the “turn-initial so” marks the 
boundary between two portions of a sequence, and the “stand-alone so” passes the turn to 
another participant, to encourage either sequence expansion or sequence closure. Then, “so” 
followed by an “uh(m)” and often a pause appears when a speaker is trying to exit or re-exit a 
sequence (Schegloff, 2009). Furthermore, the acknowledgement token “Yeah” described in 
Drummond and Hopper (1993) acknowledges the previous topic before further elaborating. 
By looking at the combination of the “so” and “yeah”, which has not yet been analyzed in 
interaction, I will argue that on the most general level, “so yeah” is used for topic management. 
In the turn-initial position, “so yeah” can be used to preface an elaboration of an ongoing topic or 
to propose a return to an agenda. In the turn-final position, or as a stand-alone TCU, “so yeah” is 
often produced less audibly and is used to (re-)exit a sequence, much like “so” followed by an 
“uh(m)”.  The “turn-final” and the “stand-alone so yeah” are often used for topic management, 
but unlike the “turn-initial so yeah,” they are used to mark the end of responses to telling 
questions, or to retract a projected continuation and pass the turn to the recipient.  
These findings add to our understanding of sequence and topic management as well as to 
what we know about the relationships between meaning and turn position.
